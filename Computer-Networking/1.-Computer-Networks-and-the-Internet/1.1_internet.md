# 1.1 What is the Internet?

> **Big Idea**: The Internet can be described in 2 ways.
> 1. **The Nuts-andBolts way** - what it's physically made of (wires, computers, routers).
> 2. **The Services way** - what it *does* for the apps and people who use it.
> Think of it like describing a car: you could describe its engine parts, **OR** you could describe what it does (gets you from home to school).

---

## 1.1.1 The Nuts-and-Bolts Description

### What is the Internet, physically?

The Internet is basically a **giant network that connects billions of devices** all around the world. It's not just computers anymore - it's phones, tablets, TVs, game consoles, thermostats, fridges, security cameras, watches, cars, and more.

### Key Vocabularies

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Host** (or **End System**) | Any device connected to the Internet - your phone, laptop, smart fridge, etc. It's called an "end system" because it's at the *end* of the network (where data starts(source: where data came from) or finishes(destination: where data is recieved) its trip.) |
| **Packet** | A small "chunk" of data. When you send something big (like a photo), the Internet chops it into smaller pieces called packets before sending it. |
| **Packet Switch** | A device that recieves packets and forwards them toward their destination - like a traffic cop directing cars at a intersection. |
| **Modem** | Translates the ISP's incoming signal into something your home devices understand (and back). It'r your bridge to the outside world. |
| **Router** | Directs packets between different networks, and lets multiple devices share one internet connection. |
| **Link-layer Switch** | Connects devices within the same local network (like all computers in one office) and only forwards data to the right device inside that network - it doesn't reach outside to the wider internet. |
| **Route (or Path)** | The trail of links and switches a packet travel through to get from sender to receiver. |
| **ISP (Internet Service Provider)** | The company that connects you to the Internet (e.g., Comcast, Verizon, your university's Wi-Fi). |

### The Truck Analogy (Very Important)

The Internet can be compared to a highway system for trucks. This comparison comes up on exams a lot.

| Internet Concept | Highway/Trucking Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Packet | Truck |
| Communication Link | Highway / Road |
| Packet Switch | Intersection |
| End system | Building (factory or warehouse) |

**How it works*:*
1. A factory (sender) has a huge shipment of cargo (data) to send.
2. The cargo gets split up and loaded onto multiple trucks (packets).
3. Each truck drives independently through roads and intersections (the network) to reach the destination warehouse (receiver).
4. At the warehouse, all the cargo gets unloaded and reassembled (packets are put back together into the original data).

## How Devices Connect: ISPs

You don't connect directly to "the Internet" - you connect through **ISP** (Internet Service Provider). Examples:
- **Residential ISPs** - your home internet company (cable, phone company, DSL).
- **Corporate ISPs** - internet provided by a workplace.
- **University ISPs** - internet provided by a college.
- **Public Wi-Fi ISPs** - airports, hotels, coffee shops.
- **Cellular ISPs** - your phone's mobile data provider.

ISPs are connected to each other too:
- **Lower-tier ISPs** (local/regional) connect up to...
- **Upper-tier ISPs** (national/global) - these are the "big highways" if the Internet, made of super-fast fiber-optic cables and high-speed routers.

> **Analogy:** Think of lower-tier ISPs as neighborhood streets, and upper-tier ISPs as magor interstate highways connecting entire regions of the country together.

### Protocols - The "Rules" Everyone Follows

- **TCP (Transmission Control Protocol)** and **IP (Internet Protocol)** are the two MOST improtant protocols on the Internet.
- **IP** decides the *format* of packets (basically, what a packet has to look like)
- Together, they're called **TCP/IP** - you'll hear this term constantly in networking.

### Standards - Making Sure Everyone Agree

- Protocols only work if Everyone follows the same rules (like a universal language).
- The **IETF (Internet Engineering Task Force)** creates official Internet standards.
- These standards are publiched as documents called **RFCs (Request for Comments)** - there are almost 9,000 of them! They define protocols like TCP, IP, HTTP (used for websites), and SMIP (used for email).
- The **IEEE 802 LAN Standards Committee** handles standards for things like Ethernet and Wi-Fi.

## 1.1.2 A Sevices Description

This is the **second way** of describing the Internet: as something that provides *services* to apps, rather than describing its physical parts.

### Distributed Applications

- Apps like email, video streaming, maps, social media, and video calls are called **distributed applications** because they involved **multiple end systems** talking to each other (e.g, your phone and a server, or you and your friends).
- **Important fact:** Application run ON end systems (your phone, a server) - they fo **NOT** run inside packet switches/routers. Routers just move the packets around: they don't care what the data actually is.

### The Socket Interface

- The **socket interface** is a set of rules that tells a program HOW to ask the Internet to deliver data to another program on a different device.
- Think of it as the "instruction manual" a program must follow to send data across the Internet.

### The Postal Service Analogy (Also Very Important)

The book compares sending data over the internet to Alice mailing a letter to Bob:

| Internet Concept | Postal Service Equivalent |
|---|---|
| Data | The letter itself |
| Socket Interface rules | Postal service rules (must use an envelope, write address, and stamp, etc.) |
| Choosing Internet service for your app. | Choosing a service (regular mail, express mail, etc.)

**Steps Alice follows to mail a letter:**
1. Write the letter (create the data)
2. Put it in an envelope.
3. Write Bob's address and zip code.
4. Seal the envelope.
5. Add a stamp.
6. Drop it in an official mailbox.

Just like Alice must follow the post office's rules, a program mist follow the **socket interface's** rules so the Internet knows where to deliver the data.

## 1.1.3 What Is a Protocol?

### Human Analogy #1: Asking for the Time

Imagine you want to ask a stranger for the time:
1. You say **"Hi"** (a greeting to start communication).
2. They say **"Hi"** back (a friendly response - this means "okay to continue").
3. You ask, **"Got the time?"**
4. They answer, **"2:00"**

If instead they respond with "Don't bother me!" or ignored you completely, you'd know NOT to ask for the time - that's part of the "protocol" too knowing when to stop.

> **Key takeaway:** A protocol isn't just about the messages - it's also about the **actions taken** based on the response (or lack of response).

### Human Analogy #2: Classroom Question

1. Teacher asks, **"Any questions?"** (message sent to everyone)
2. You raise your hand (a signal/message back to the teacher)
3. Teacher says **Yes...?"** (acknowledges you, gives permission to continue)
4. You ask your question (message sent)
5. Teacher answers (message sent back)

### Network Protocol Example: Loading a Webpage

This mirrors the human "Hi / Got the time?" example exactly, but between computers:

1. Your computer sends a **connection request** to the web server ("Hi").
2. The web server sends back a **connection reply** ("Hi" back)
3. Your computer sends a **GET message** aksing for a specific webpage ("Got the time?")
4. The web server sends back the **webpage/file** ("2:00")

### Official Definition of Protocol (Important)
> **A protocol defines the FORMAT and ORDER of messages exchanged between two or more communicating entities, as well as the ACTIONS taken on the transmission and/or receipt of a message of other event.**

In plain English: **A protocol is a set of agreed-upon rules for HOW and WHEN devices talk to each other and WHAT to do based on what's said (or not said).**


### Protocol vs. Socket Interface - The Difference

- The **socket interface** represents the rules for how a program talks to its own computer to send/receive data. (local hand-off, program <-> its own machine)
- A **protocol** represents the rules for how one computer talks to another computer once the data is out on the network. (remote conversation, machine <-> another machine)

## Quick Review — Key Takeaways

- The Internet = billions of connected devices (hosts/end systems) linked by communication links and packet switches.
- Data is broken into **packets**, sent independently, and reassembled at the destination — just like trucks carrying cargo through highways.
- **ISPs** connect end systems to the Internet, and ISPs themselves connect to each other in tiers (lower-tier → upper-tier).
- **TCP/IP** are the Internet's most important protocols.
- The **IETF** creates standards called **RFCs**.
- The Internet can ALSO be described as a service provider for apps — this is the **services description**.
- The **socket interface** is the "rulebook" apps use to send/receive data, just like the postal service has rules for mailing letters.
- A **protocol** = rules about message format, order, and what actions to take (like human conversation etiquette, but for machines).
