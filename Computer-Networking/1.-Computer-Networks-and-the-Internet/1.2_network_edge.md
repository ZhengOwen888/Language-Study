# 1.2 The Network Edge

> **Big Idea**: Last Section gave us a bird's-eye view of the whole Internet. Now we zoom into the **"edge"** - the outermost part of the network, meaning the devices and connections closest to YOU (as opposed to the "core," which is the big backbone in the middle).

> **Analogy:** Think of the Internet like a river system. The "edge" is like the small streams and creeks near people's houses (where water enters/exits). The "core" is like the giant main river foing the heavy lifting in the middle. This section is all about the streams.

---

## Setting the Scene: End Systems at the Edge

- Recall from 1.1: devices connected to the Internet = **end systems** (or **hosts**).
- They're called "end systems" because they literally sit at the **edge/end** of the network - the network doesn't go any further than them.
- Examples: desktops, Macs, Linux boxes, servers (Web/email servers), laptops, smartphones, tablets, and more and more "smart things" (thermostats, watches, etc).

### Hosts = Clients OR Servers

| Type | What it usually is | What it does |
|---|---|---|
| **Client** | Desktops, laptops, smartphones | The device YOU use to request stuff (load a webpage, check email) |
| **Server** | More powerful, dedicated machines | Stores/distributes content - servers web pages, streams video, relays email |

> **Important fact:** Most servers today (that gives you search results, email, videos, etc.) live inside massive **data centers**, not just one lonely computer somewhere. (Example: as of 2020, Google alone had 19 data centers across 4 continents with millions of servers combined)

---

## Case History: Data Centers and Cloud Computing

Big companies (Google, Microsoft, Amazon, Alibaba) build **massive data centers*** - buildings packed with tens to hundreds of THOUSANDS of hosts.

### The 3 Jobs a Data Center Does (using Amazon as the example):
1. **Serves regular content** - like Amazon's shopping pages and product info.
2. **Runs internal heavy-duty computing** - Amazon's own big data-processing tasks.
3. **Provides cloud computing to OTHER companies** - e.g., Air bnb doesn't own its own data centers: it rents computing power from Amazon Web Services (AWS) instead.

### Key Vocabulary

| Term | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Blade** | An ultra-thin, modular computer. To save space, it strips away its individual power cord and cooling fans, relying entirely on a shread housing (chassis) to turn on and stay cool. |
| **Rack** | A standardized metal frame or wardrobe-like cabinet. It does no computing itself, its only job is to stack servers vertically to save floor space and organize cables. |
| **Cloud Computing** | Renting computing power/storage from a big company's data center instead of owning your own servers. |

## 1.2.1 Access Networks

### What is an Access Network?

> **Acess Network = the network that physically connects an end system to the FIRST router** on its path to anywhere else on the Internet. This first router is also called the **"edge router"**.

> **Analogy:** Think of the access network as your home's driveway connecting to the very first street outside your house. It's the "on-ramp" to the rest of the highway system (the Internet).

There are 3 broad settings for access networks:
1. **Home access** - DSL, Cable, FTTH, 5G Fixed Wireless.
2. **Enterprise/campus access** (and increasingly home too) - Ethernet and Wi-Fi.
3. **Wide-area mobile wireless access** - 3G/4G/5G cellular

---

### Home Access Networks

#### DSL (Digital Subscriber Line)

- Provided by your **phone company (telco)** - so your telco doubles your ISP.
- Uses your **existing telephone wires**.
- Splits the SAME wire into 3 "virtual" channels using different frequencies, so data and voice calls can travel on the same line at the same time without interfering:

| Channel | Frequency Range | Purpose |
|---|---|---|
| Downstream (data coming TO you) | 50 kHz - 1 MHz | Fastest - for receiving data |
| Upstream (data going FROM you) | 4 kHz - 50 kHz | Medium speed - for sending data |
| Telephone | 0 - 4 kHz | Regular phone calls |

- **DSL Modem** (at home) <-> **DSLAM** (Digital Subscriber Line Access Multiplexer, at the phone company's Central Office) - these two "talk" to each other to move your data.
- A **splitter** at your house seperates the phone signal from the data signal.
- **Asymmetric access** = downstream speed != upstream speed (download is faster than upload). DSL is asymmetric.
- DSL has a distance limit - generally needs to be within 5 - 10 miles of the phone company's office, or it won't work well.

#### Cable Internet

- Provided by your **cable TV company**, using the SAME infrastructure as cable television.
- Uses **Hybrid Fiber Coax (HFC)** - fiber-optic cable for the long stretch, then regular coaxial cable for the final stretch into houses.
- **Cable Modem** (at home) <-> **CMTS** (Cable Modem Termination System, at the "cable head end") - same idea as DSL's modem/DSLAM pair, just different names.
- Also **asymmetric** (faster download, slower upload).
- **Important:** Cable is a **SHARED media** - meaning your neighbors share the SAME cable line as you. If lots of neighbots are streaming video at once, your speed can slow down because you're all splitting the same pipe. DSL does NOT have this problem because your line is dedicated just to you.

#### FTTH (Fiber to the Home)

- Runs an actual **optical fiber cable** all the way from the phone company's office to your house - the fastest home option, can reach **gigabits per second**.
- Key parts:

| Part | What it does|
|---|---|
| **ONT** (Optical Network Terminator) | The box at YOUR home that connects to the fiber. |
| **Splitter** | Combines/splits fiber signal so multiple homes can share one main fiber line. |
| **OLT** (Optical line Terminator) | The box back at the phone company's Central Office that connects the fiber network to the Internet. |

#### 5G Fixed Wireless

- A newer option - sends internet **wirelessly** from the provider's base station straight to a modem at your home (no need to physically run new cable to the house).
- Users "beam-forming" technology to aim the wireless signal directly at your home.

---

### Enterprise and Home Access: Ethernet and Wi-Fi

#### Ethernet
- The MOST common LAN (Local Area Network) technology in offices, schools, and homes.
- Uses **twisted-pair copper wire** to connect devices to an **Ethernet switch**.
- Typical speeds: users get 100 Mbps-tens of Gbps, servers often get 1-10 Gbps.

### Wi-Fi
- Based on the **IEEE 802.11** standard.
- Devices connect wirelessly to an **access point**, which is wired into the larger network (usually via Ethernet).
- You generally need to be within a few tens of meters of the access point.
- Found basically everywhere now: homes, schools, cafes, airports, even planes.
- Shared transmission rate: over 100 Mbps (shared among everyone connected to that access point).

#### Typical Home Network Setup
A modern home network usually combines:
- A broadband connection (cable or DSL modem) coming in from outside.
- A **home router** connecting everything to the Internet.
- A wireless **access point / base station** for Wi-Fi devices.
- Wired devices (like a desktop PC) plugged in directly.

---

### Wide-Area Wireless Access: 3G, 4G, 5G

- Uses the SAME cellular network as your phone calls to send/recieve Internet data.
- Connects through a **base station** run by your cellular provider.
- **Key difference from Wi-Fi:** cellular range is much bigger - you can be **tens of KILOMETERS** away from the base station (vs. Wi-Fi's tens of METERS).
- **4G** -> real-world download speeds up to ~60Mbps.
- **5G** -> newer, even faster, currently being rolled out.

## 1.2.2 Physical Media

> **Physical Medium** = the actual physical "stuff" that data travels through/over as electromagnetic waves or light pulses.
- Like the actual pavement under the highway.

Every bit of data, on its journey from sender to receiver, passes through MANY transmitter -> receiver pairs (like relay racers passing a baton), and each "leg" of that journey can use a different type of physical medium.

### Two Big Categories
| Category | Definition | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Guided Media** | Waves travel through a solid, physical path | Fiber-optic cable, twisted-pair copper wire, coaxial cable |
| **Unguided Media** | Waves travel freely trough the air/space - nothing "guides" them | Wireless LAN (Wi-Fi), satellite signals |

> **Cost note:** the materials (wire/cable) are usually CHEAP. The expensive part is the LABOR to install it. That's why builders often install multiple types of cable in walls at once - it's cheaper to do it all now than to pay a worker to come back later.

### Breakfown of Each Medium

| Medium | Type | Key Facts |
|---|---|---|
| **Twisted-Pair Copper Wire** | Guided | Cheapest and most common. Used for over 100 years in telephone lines. Two insulated copper wires twisted together to reduce interference. Modern versions (Cat 6a) reach 10 Gbps up to ~100 meters. Used for DSL and Ethernet LANS. |
| **Coaxial Cable** | Guided | Two copper conductors, but arraged as circles-within-circles (concentric) instead of side-by side. Common in cable TV. Can be a **shared medium** - multiple end systems can be connected to and receive from the same cable. |
| **Fiber Optics** | Guided | Thin, flexible strand that carries pulses of LIGHT (not electricity) — each light pulse = one bit. Extremely fast (tens-hundreds of Gbps), immune to electrical interference, very low signal loss over distance (works well up to 100 km!), hard to tap into (good for security). Expensive equipment though, so it's mostly used for long-distance/backbone connections rather than short home hookups. |
| **Terrestrial Radio Channels** | Unguided | No physical wire needed — travels through open air. Can penetrate walls and reach moving/mobile users. Signal strength affected by distance, obstacles, and interference. Grouped into: very short-range (headsets, keyboards), local-area (WiFi), and wide-area (cellular). |
| **Satellite Radio Channels** | Unguided | Ground stations send signals up to a satellite, which regenerates and re-sends them back down (possibly to a totally different location). Two types below. |

### Satellite Types

| Type | Distance from Earth | Key Trait |
|---|---|---|
| **Geostationary** | ~36,000 km up | Stays fixed above ONE spot on Earth. Has a noticeable delay (~280 milliseconds) because of the huge distance the signal has to travel. Used in areas with no DSL/cable access. |
| **LEO (Low-Earth Orbiting)** | Much closer to Earth | Constantly moving around the Earth (like the Moon does). Needs MANY satellites working together to cover one area continuously. Still an emerging/developing technology for Internet access. |

## Quick Review — Key Takeaways (Section 1.2)

- The **network edge** = the end systems (hosts) closest to the user — clients and servers.
- Most modern servers live inside **data centers**, which also provide **cloud computing** (e.g., AWS).
- An **access network** connects an end system to its first ("edge") router.
- Home access options: **DSL** (via telco, uses phone lines, NOT shared), **Cable** (via cable company, uses HFC, IS shared), **FTTH** (fiber all the way home, fastest), **5G Fixed Wireless** (no cable install needed).
- **Asymmetric access** = download speed ≠ upload speed (true for DSL & cable).
- **Ethernet** (wired) and **WiFi** (wireless, IEEE 802.11) dominate enterprise/campus/home LANs.
- Cellular (3G/4G/5G) access uses the same infrastructure as phone calls, with MUCH greater range than WiFi.
- Physical media = **guided** (wire/cable/fiber — physical path) vs. **unguided** (radio/satellite — travels through open air/space).
- Fiber optics = fastest, most reliable, but priciest to install — used for long-distance/backbone links.
- Coaxial cable = shared medium (can cause slowdowns with more users); twisted pair = NOT shared, dedicated per line.
- Satellites: **Geostationary** = fixed position but noticeable delay; **LEO** = closer, moving, needs multiple satellites for full coverage.
